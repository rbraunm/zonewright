# Building a zone

How a zone is built with zonewright, and what the tools need to support it. A zone is not built in one pass: each part goes from rough to refined to detailed, and every pass is followed by looking at the result and deciding what to change, including taking work back out.

## Principles

- **Intent before tools, region by region.** Each area of a zone is planned for what it is to become, from the concept and references, and kept as a region with its intent. Shaping, surfacing, and dressing are then done region by region at any scale. A slope or height recipe belongs to an area, as the client's terrain ecosystems do: they put rock on steep ground across most of a zone, but each painted area has its own palette and thresholds, height bands target features, and edges blend softly. Noise and scatter likewise work inside a chosen area. One hard rule with one palette across the whole zone reads as generated.
- **Steer by the EQ worlds.** How the client's own zones are built is measured (the zone survey's `construction` group) and the zone in progress is compared with them after each pass (`compareWithClientZones`), so judgment rests on EQ's practice rather than on taste alone.
- **Coarse to fine.** Each pass works at its own scale and resolution: big forms on a coarse grid, then refinement, then breakup and detail. Nothing is detailed before its position and shape are settled, because detail on something that moves is wasted.
- **Look after every pass.** Building a zone is visual iteration: a pass is judged by looking at pictures of it, from where players stand (eye-height views along the routes), from above (layout), and close on the part being worked from several sides, against the reference and against EQ scale (a 6-unit character, walkable slopes, run distances). Measurements check what a picture shows; they never replace it. The judgment decides the next pass, which may be a correction, a reduction, or a removal.
- **Passes stay adjustable.** A terrain pass can be turned up, turned down, or removed after later passes are made, so a bad pass costs one change, not a rebuild. Arrangements keep their transforms as data and can be regenerated or re-settled after the ground under them changes.
- **Removing is a way of adding.** Carving a path, lowering a basin, deleting a face, or dialing a noise pass back to half are as normal as raising and placing. Shapes often read better after something is taken away.
- **Layers of the zone are independent.** Terrain shape, surfaces, and placed objects change separately: re-sculpting re-seats the props on it, and retexturing does not touch shape.

## Passes

| Pass | Purpose | Works on |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Layout | The playable space at gameplay scale: size, routes, landmarks, zone lines, sightlines; then the regions, each with its intent. Fast and disposable. | A coarse terrain grid (about 32 units), route paths, proxy blocks for landmarks and buildings, regions |
| 2. Primary forms | Hills, valleys, ridges, cliffs, basins, and the routes graded into them; proxies replaced by building masses | Terrain at its working resolution (8 to 16 units), large brushes |
| 3. Secondary forms | Breaking regularity: no perfect cones or circles, uneven ridgelines, gullies down slopes, ledges, rock outcrops | Warp and medium-scale noise in their own passes; outcrops placed and sunk |
| 4. Surfacing | Materials painted by intent, region by region: each region's ground, strata, paths, accents; edges shaped deliberately; transitions between them; texture scale kept even | Surfacing layers painted by region and stroke, edge edits, UV projection |
| 5. Dressing | Props and clusters that make places: explicit sets placed by hand and generated sets (rows, rings, scatter) with varied scale, heading, and tilt | Linked copies of models and kit assets, settled on the ground |
| 6. Detail | Fine breakup on terrain away from routes; small props and debris | Fine noise passes masked by slope and distance from routes |
| 7. Review | Problems (floating or buried props, unwalkable route slopes, texture stretch), budgets, export round trip | Checks and views |

Any pass can send the work back to an earlier one.

## What the tools need

### Regions

A region is a vertical prism over an outline with its intent written on it (`createRegion`): "north guild terrace: packed earth, dwellings carved into the back wall". Regions are seen in Blender and never rendered or exported. The `{"region": name}` selector confines every tool to one, so shaping, surfacing, and dressing happen inside a decided area.

### Sketches

An artist sketches a layout before and while building it, to see what might work: where buildings stand and face, the yards and lanes between them, the arrival point, a dungeon's rooms. `sketch` draws shapes on named sheets (areas, footprints with heights and facings, paths with widths, points, notes) and measures each against the ground under it (its slope, cut and fill to its floor, water, overlaps and gaps to its neighbors), and `renderSketch` draws the sheets with the regions, plots, and water over a grey relief map. A footprint with a height stands in views as a plain block, so a layout can be judged at eye height before anything is built. Sketches stay in the file and are revised like any other work, but they are aids, not the plan: nothing holds the zone to them, export leaves them out, and players do not stand on them. Reach for them when the next step or how to improve an area is unclear.

### Surfacing layers

A terrain's face materials are composed from named, ordered layers (`addSurfaceLayer`): each face shows the topmost unmuted layer covering it, else the materials it had before layering. A layer is painted by region, along a stroke, or at a point (`paintSurface`), with noise on its edge so it wanders as a painted edge does; its edges are grown, shrunk, smoothed, or cleaned of small islands and holes (`editSurface`); it is erased, muted, reordered, or removed to take a decision back. This matches how most of the client's EQG terrains are surfaced: per-face materials in clean regions whose borders follow modeled edges. A painted border can only follow the mesh's edges, so it is brought onto edges along a smooth line (`conformSurfaceEdges`: each border evened along its length, keeping the area it encloses, and vertices off creases slid along their edges onto it, in every shaping pass, carrying UVs; a stroke whose pieces touch at a corner is evened as one; a border it evened stays as it is when run again at that smoothing or less, until paint beside it changes), and where a band or strip must end at a set height or distance from a border, the faces are cut there first (`cutContours`, which also cuts an edge the level crosses twice, as on a wall one cell wide). A transition texture goes on as a strip along a border (`paintTransition`), its bottom edge on the border and repeating along it; at a wall's foot it measures from the foot only (`onlyAbove`, and the same on the cut), notches of ground poking up the wall and all, and spans its mapping smoothly out from the border so it does not smear over a notch. The strip keeps its own mapping in its layer, so erasing, muting, or removing it leaves the mapping beneath as it was. Cleaning a layer (`editSurface` clean) lifts its own specks off or fills its holes with its own paint and never copies another layer's material into it.

### Taking an area back

`resetRegion` takes shaping passes back inside an area, faded at its edge; `rebuildRegion` spans the area's heights smoothly from the ground around it (a blank slate that already fits) or levels it, laying its vertices out again where the base has them so sideways moves of earlier passes leave no creases, and turning its diagonals to the new ground; `clearRegion` does both kinds at once along with erasing its surfacing (given the edgeNoise the region was painted with, also the paint that spilled past its edge, and any paint left within the noise's reach of it cut off from paint beyond, so the spill goes even after reshaping) and deleting the objects placed in it. Every EQG zone has one terrain mesh, so areas are reworked inside it rather than split into separate meshes.

Passes hold moves, not shapes. A later pass that raised ground to a level (a fill's plateau, a terrace) holds the lift it needed over the passes beneath, so taking back an earlier pass under it moves that level: a plateau built on a hill dishes where the hill is taken back. `resetRegion` names the kept passes still shaping the area (`stillShapedBy`); level the plateau again (`rebuildRegion` height) or take that pass back too.

### Recovering from a wreck

Work goes forward: passes, layers, and the region tools take a decision back without losing what came after it. Checkpoints are for when something goes badly wrong (a broad change that wrecks the terrain, a bad script): `saveCheckpoint` before a risky change saves the work file and keeps a copy of it under the tooling root, never in the work repository, and `restoreCheckpoint` puts one back, first keeping the current state as a checkpoint so a restore can itself be taken back. A checkpoint goes back only over the work file it came from, since that file's textures are on paths relative to it. `listCheckpoints` gives their sizes; nothing deletes one but `deleteCheckpoints`, by name.

### Measured against the client

The zone survey's `construction` group measures every client zone: terrain triangle density, textures on the terrain, the share of terrain triangles in one- or two-triangle material islands, steep share, steep area on terrain rather than placed models, painted share, and placements. `compareWithClientZones` measures the open scene the same way and places each measure among chosen client zones by percentile. The `author-zone` skill lists the EQG distributions.

### Terrain passes

Each shaping operation writes into a named pass on the terrain (a Blender shape key over the terrain's base shape). Passes are listed, and each can be set to any strength, muted, removed, or collapsed into the base. Export draws the terrain as the passes combine. A change of resolution (subdividing) collapses the passes first, since a pass holds one offset per vertex.

- **Rough:** large forms from a few inputs: a mound or basin at a point, a ridge or valley along a path with a cross-section profile, a plateau.
- **Refine:** the point and path brushes (raise, lower, smooth, flatten, crease, carve), and grading a route so it reads as a path (`gradeRoute`: a bench level across, graded between the heights given, with landings at hairpins and batters meeting the ground; the artist places a switchback's hairpins, the tool never adds one).
- **Breakup:** warp (moving vertices sideways by smooth noise, so round shapes stop being round) and roughen (fractal noise up and down, by feature size, amplitude, and octaves), each in its own pass and limited by a mask.
- **Masks** shared by every terrain and surfacing tool: slope range, height range, distance from a route, regions, and the existing selectors; they pick within an area chosen by intent.

The usual sequence for natural ground: rough forms, look, warp, look, medium roughen, look, smooth where it went too far, carve routes and channels, fine roughen masked off the routes, look. Any pass that does not help is turned down or removed.

Some passes are defined: a graded route and a dressed facade (below) each keep their definition in their own pass, and the plots graded on a terrain keep theirs on the plots and are graded together, as one feature placed at the first of their passes. A defined pass is rebuilt whole from its definition against the ground under it, so it holds the move from that ground to its target and takes no other shaping. Each is graded on the ground without its own pass and without any defined pass made after it, so where two meet (a route arriving at a plot's entrance, two routes crossing) the later one wins. Grading one again replays every later one whose ground it changed; other shaping made later (a roughen, a sculpt) leaves the defined passes off target until `regradeTerrain` grades them all again in the order they were made, and then cuts again every cave whose ground moved. `getObjectDetail` names the defined passes and caves whose ground moved since they were made, an export lists them to confirm, and a game export refuses them. Look at the ground after a regrade as after any pass.

### Terrain that is not a heightfield

EQG terrain is a mesh, not a height grid, so a zone can have near-vertical walls, overhangs, caves, and arches, and the tools have to make them:

- **Resolution where it is needed:** a grid stretched down a steep wall becomes a few long triangles, so steep areas are refined (more vertices on the wall) before they are shaped.
- **Shaping along the surface:** brushes, warp, and roughen move vertices along the surface normal, which on a wall is sideways: that is how ledges, alcoves, and overhangs are made, and how cliffs get breakup.
- **Caves:** cut into the terrain itself along a floor path (`cutCave`), as the client's own are built: a tube with a flat floor, straight walls, and a vault, a room a wide stretch of it, its walls broken up by noise that fades out at the mouth so the lip reads as a clean arch where the outside rock turns to the inside rock. A covered gallery or rock shelter is the same tube run along a cliff with part of its width in the open (`traceLedge` traces its path a set share inside the rock, graded evenly), surfaced close in value to the cliff's own rock so it reads as a ledge under a lip rather than a stripe. A floor whose middle would hang in the air is refused. Every slope and level is the author's decision, made for what the space is for: a run climbs at a chosen grade because it leads up to a room, or at the even grade between the heights given; a bend named a landing turns level, as a builder lands a switchback; a hall is level because it holds plots. The floor follows the same rule: named floor strokes shape it by intent and come back on every cut, a level way held exactly at the floor through rubble painted either side (in rock or scree of its own, broken into crests, its foot wandering, heaped up a wall only where the author banks it), a pad set level at a height for a plot, a dais, or a terrace step, each with straight edges; with none, the floor stays level wall to wall on purpose. A plot in a cave stands on a level way or pad, at its height, and `placePlot` and `assessPlot` name it. A cave branches where the author says: a side passage, a side room, a fork, or a second mouth is a branch, a run of its own leaving the main run or another branch at the author's point and angle, on its parent's floor or high in its wall as an overlook; all runs are cut at once, so the junction is one clean opening, framed for a portal piece as Underquarry frames its doorways. Rooms stack as the client's do: a run may climb to a room over another, pass under a hall, or spiral round a shaft, and separate caves may pass inside the rock, as long as `minimumRock` of rock stays between them; mouths stay apart. Caves are lit as the client's are: their geometry takes a share of scene light (daylight) set from what the mouth lets in, full at the mouth and none past the first bend, so a cave is not lit like the cliff outside nor moonlit all the way in; lamps are `LIB_` point lights anchored on the lining (`placeLights` onCave), warm lamp light with a cool fill as Underquarry pairs them, kept to radii inside their rooms since live light reaches through rock. A cave's profile (its section along its run's centerline, unrolled, which `cutCave` and `editCave` return and `renderSection` draws for a cave's run) shows the climb, its grades, the landings, the vault, and the ground over it in one picture; plans (`renderSketch`) draw each run's walls and floor heights, and `walkRoute` walks a run. Where rock lies over ground, a cave under a hill, the tools that find ground say which: a plot is placed with its height (a level stretch of the cave gives its floor) and is measured from it, a dropped object lands on the floor under the rock over it, and scattering asks for the height to drop from. The hill over a room stays a separate sheet of the same mesh, and the mouth is welded into the ground around it. The cut keeps every shaping pass: the ground faces it took out are recorded and their vertices stay, so taking the cave back (`removeCave`) puts the ground back exactly; the vertices where it meets the ground follow that ground through every pass, so shaping around the mouth never opens a seam; and its lining holds still. Tools that would change faces at a cave refuse (take it back, change, cut it again), and surface tools leave its lining alone unless their selector names the cave. Once the ground within its reach moves, the cave is flagged stale and is cut again to fit (`editCave`, or `regradeTerrain` for every cave). A cave is judged as any form is: from outside the mouth and close on its throat, from inside looking out, in the room with the figure, from the hill above (which shows no trace of it), and in sections across and along.
- **Arches and bridges:** what is left of the layered rock where the gorge cut under it, not something laid across: the top is the plateau's own surface, the sides are the gorge walls carried on, the strata run through it and into the walls, and the opening is cut from below as one broad curve, the lintel thickening into the abutments. It is its own piece in the terrain, so it is walked on and exported as ground, with its hidden parts running into the rock around it.

A pass holds one offset per vertex, so tools that add or remove vertices otherwise (refining, extruding, boolean cuts, joining) first collapse the passes into the base; cuts along a line (`cutContours`, the waterline cut `carveWaterBed` makes) and turned diagonals keep them, each new vertex placed alike in every pass. A cave keeps them too, placing its own vertices in every pass; while a mesh holds caves, the tools that change faces refuse it and cuts and turned diagonals keep clear of the caves, since taking a cave back depends on the faces around it staying as they were cut. That fits coarse to fine: forms are settled on the coarse grid, collapsed, refined, and shaped further in new passes.

### Water

Water is laid in body by body, each a named object rebuilt from what it was made from whenever it changes, against the ground as it is then: a pool floods from a point to a level over the ground below it; a river follows a path of falling levels over the ground below its level within reach of the path, cut square at its ends; a fall hangs from a lip, turning over the edge and arcing out as it drops. Their surfaces reach a little under their banks so no seam shows at the waterline, and where a bound crosses open water (a river's reach, a within outline, a stroke) the surface is cut cleanly along it. Plans, sections, and the reported extent show the water players see, not the surface tucked under the banks. A broad tool makes a starting point (a flood, a river along a whole channel); strokes and edits shape it (a removed stroke across a leak, a raised level, a moved lip), with a look after each; a stroke that changes nothing yet is kept and says why, so one can be laid ahead of the edit it is for (a removed stroke across a side channel before the level is raised). The bed is shaped after: carving it cuts the ground along the waterline first, so the shore stays put, and `cutContours` with a waterline cuts that line and a bank band's edge without carving, on a sloping river as on a pool; the bed and bank selectors take whole faces, so they meet those lines exactly once they are cut. Where players swim is designed apart from the surfaces, once the water and its bed settle: swim volumes are boxes (the `.zon`'s `AWT_` water and `ALV_` lava regions) that a generator starts for a body (`buildSwimVolumes`) and an artist adjusts or places by hand, and they need not match any surface (a floating pool is design). Export writes them as they stand and derives none; a body changed since its boxes were accepted, or with no boxes and not marked not swimmable, is listed by a test export and refused by a game export, and a box left without water over it is a finding. A body marked not swimmable has no boxes: export refuses any tied to one. Sections (`renderSection`) show boxes against the surface and the bed; views (`renderView` with swim volumes) tint what the boxes cover, seen through the water but not through the ground. The surfaces export with the client's own water, waterfall, and lava shaders.

Water moves as the client's does, by its material's slides along the body's own texture coordinates ([clientRendering.md](clientRendering.md#eqg-zones)): a fall spans its lip once (`acrossRepeats` for a fall of side-by-side strands) so a fall texture's faded edges are its sides, and runs down the drop; a river runs downstream. `createLiquidMaterial`'s `flow` sets slides that move both layers along the body (a river, water whose layers move one way, or a waterfall ribbon laid over a rock bed as Crescent Reach's rivers are); left still, water churns in place as the client's pools do. `getWater` says which way each body flows and how fast, so slides that run a river upstream show at once, and `renderView` at two `liquidTime`s a second apart shows it. White water is not a surface: the client's falls and rivers have none. It is particle emitters where the water strikes, sprayed onto a body with `sprayWater` (a fall's foot or lip, or points laid by hand on a pool or river: rocks, steps, rapids scattered where the water breaks, a river's join with a pool), with ripple rings on the water beside them, chosen from the definitions the client's own falls use, and kept with the body, so a rebuilt, moved, or copied body carries its spray along and a fall's foot follows the pool or river it lands in when that is rebuilt or deleted, an emitter nudged or hidden by hand staying so while the water under it holds still; and wet rocks placed by hand at a fall's foot (`placeObject`), which hide where the sheet meets the water. A fall is looked at from across its pool, at eye level at its foot, from above, and close on the white water, by day and by night (particles draw in their own colors, not the scene's light, so mist stays bright at night).

### Player boundaries

How far players may go is designed, never derived, as the client's own zones do it: a rim taller than eye height around the play area, invisible walls at its foot and along any open edge, lids over canyon paths players could climb out of, and zone lines in the gaps the walls leave. A wall (`placeBoundaryWall`) is a ribbon along a path from 5 under the ground to a height above it, as the client's invw walls run, and follows the ground as it is when placed; placing it again redraws it after the ground changes. A lid or a floor (`placeBoundaryPlane`) is a flat outline at a height. Boundaries are never drawn in the client's view; views with guides tint them red in every shading, plans and sections draw them, `walkRoute` is stopped by them from either side, and export merges them into the terrain as triangles without a material, which the client collides with but never draws, a wall facing both ways so it blocks whichever side its path left it facing. Players pass through liquid surfaces and cutout cards, and through anything marked passable (`markPassable`): art with its own collision shell, hanging moss. A zone line (`placeZoneLine`) is an axis-aligned `ATP_<number>_<label>` box in a gap of the walls, drawn green with guides, with where it leads: a zone, a point, and a heading, each part of the point and the heading kept from the player's own when marked "keep"; a line that leads back into its own zone is a teleport or a fall catcher. The zone's safe point and underworld (`setZoneProperties`) say where players arrive and how far they may fall. A game export requires all of it; a test export writes what is designed and lists what is missing.

### Housing

A zone decides its housing before any plot exists: whether it has any, how central it is, what it is for, where the server hosts it (the public zone or instanced neighborhoods), how many plots, how they are priced, and its main routes. Plots are then placed one at a time, each an outline with the client's own border model as players will see it, graded level in its own shaping pass (every plot on a ground graded together, so none disturbs another's pad and a bank too steep between two is reported), assessed where it lies (the ground, drops and walls around it, water, view, seclusion, how much of the main routes see it), and priced from the zone's rules and its features. Export writes the zone's housing file in the shape Peridot's housing reads (plots, their border doors, prices, capacities) and lists the border models' archive for the zone.

### Arrangement

Props are set as an artist sets them, and every set stays a list of copies to edit one by one.

- **Explicit sets** (`placeCopies`): one call places many linked copies of a model or kit asset, each with its own position, rotation, and uniform scale, which are exactly what an EQ placement holds.
- **Generated sets** (`generateCopies`): rows (between two points, by count or spacing), grids, rings, and routes, with seeded jitter for heading, tilt, scale, and position; copies can face along the row or route, or out of or into the ring (EQ models face +X). The result is the same per-copy list. Scatter over a region stays with `scatterInRegion`.
- **Settling** (`settleObjects`): copies drop onto whatever is below them from above the whole scene (or, where rock lies over them, from their own tops or from just under the rock, so a column up to a hall's ceiling lands on the hall's floor), by footprint rather than by origin: sunk to the lowest ground under the footprint so no edge floats, or rested on a named object with no vertex below its surface (crates on a table, or on a ramp); then optionally deeper. A tilt share leans each copy that far toward the slope under it, keeping its heading exactly, and replaces random tilt. Settling again after the ground changes puts everything back on it.

### Structures

Built structures follow the client: big walk-on structures unique to one place are terrain (carved halls, below), and freestanding and repeated pieces (wall runs, buildings, columns) are placed models, the same piece placed again and again, turned only about the vertical and never scaled. Those come from kits, made once and placed by hand.

- **Kits.** A kit is a .blend of pieces, one file per family (`eqzones\library\kits\<kitName>.blend`: `hpTimber` for decks, posts, beams, rails, and rope; `canyonAdobe` for walls, doors, windows, corners, pillars, floors, and roofs). Each piece is a collection marked as an asset whose name starts with the file's stem, holding its meshes and a record: its kind, sockets, whether players pass through it, and the openings cut in it. Its origin is its base center, its front faces +Y, and its length runs along X. A piece is modeled to the client's sizes (`createKitPiece`: a 25 module, 12.5 and 50 too, 30 a storey, about 10 thick), each part of it (a wall's face and edge, a floor's top and edge) in a catalog texture at the client's repeat for that part (walls about 12.5, floorboards 10 to 12.5, beams and trims 2.5 to 5, wood grain along a beam, plank, or rail), or modeled by hand and marked (`markKitPiece`); doors and windows are cut through a wall piece, framed in a trim material (`cutOpening`, a door about 10 x 16); roofs are pieces of their own over a footprint (`addRoof`, shingles level along the eaves). A piece reports where its texture would show a seam against the next one. Kits are edited in their own file, and every zone that links them shows the change when next opened.
- **Sockets.** A socket is a point of a piece where another meets it, facing out: a wall's start and end at its base ends, its top; a post's base and top; a corner's turned ends. Two placed sockets at one place facing each other are joined; joins are worked out when asked, never stored.
- **Free runs.** `placeKitPiece` places one piece at a time: at a point and facing, settled onto the ground by its footprint, or snapped to a placed piece's socket, which lays runs end to end exactly a module apart (Freeport's are 25.0) and turns the next piece round a corner piece. Each placement lists its sockets, joined or free, so the next step is plain, and a piece not settled says what ground lies under it and how far it floats, so a run snapped out over a drop shows it. Pieces turn only about the vertical and keep scale 1, as the client's placements do. `swapKitPiece` puts a variant of the same sockets in place (a window or door section for a plain one) without breaking the run. A run is judged from the plaza at eye height with the figure, from above in the objects shading (one color a piece), close on each joint from both faces, and a swap with the same cameras before and after.
- **Prefabs.** A building is pieces placed in its kit file (walls snapped round corner pieces, floors inside with their tops at the walls' base, a threshold under each door, a roof from `addRoof` over the walls placed at its plate) and gathered into parts (`assemblePrefab`): exterior, interior, roof, as the client splits its buildings into models placed together. Each part is one model shared by every placement; the building's floor is its walls' base, its footprint the hull of what stands on the floor (not the eaves), and a walk-in building (an NPC's shop) names its entrances, each facing out of its doorway. It is reworked by placing, deleting, and assembling again; swapping a part's piece measures its footprint again, and the buildings placed from it go stale to be laid again.
- **Spans.** A bridge, a flight of stairs, or a walkway is one artist action laid as one model, as the client's whole-span bridges are: `buildBridge` between two anchors picked on the ground (grade the abutments first), straight, sagging about 9 percent of the span as the client's rope bridges do, or arched, decked with planks across or a floor swept along it, posts and anchors, rail bars at the figure's waist or cutout ropes following the deck and hanging plumb, on posts on their sides the whole span, trestle bents to the ground; `buildStairs` between a foot and a head, risers of 1.0 at about 21 degrees as the client's timber flights; `buildWalkway` along points, level landings at the turns, deck legs graded between, steep legs listed as flights, on stilts rising into rail posts (a corner post on the miter, standing out from both edges) or bracketed from its far edge into the cliff beside it, its ends free to stand over water or air. Rails and ropes are passable as the client's are, so an open side can be fallen from. A span meant to be ground (Crescent's walkways) goes into the terrain collection and exports as terrain. Each tool lays the pieces of its one thing by the rule the call states (a plank depth, a riser, a spacing) and refuses rather than improvises where the rule cannot hold: a deck steeper than its maximum names the largest sag that fits, a deck through a hill names where, a flight too steep names the run it needs.
- **Walls.** `buildWall` lays kit sections end to end along a path the artist draws, each leg filled exactly with the fewest modules (a leg the modules cannot fill names the lengths that would and how far to move its end), posts at joints or turns, variants (a window, a door) by section index. On a slope the sections are sheared as Freeport's are, verticals vertical, every foot under the ground, sections of one rise sharing one model, and on level ground the wall stays level; a section that would shear over 30 degrees is refused, as no client wall runs its courses up so steep a slope, and stepped level at each joint is the alternative. Each leg comes with a view from the ground in front of it.
- **Placed buildings.** A building placed in a zone (`placePrefab`) is a structure: laid from its definition (kit, prefab, site, facing, plinth) into a collection of its own, every lookup of the ground it made kept. It seats its floor on the highest ground under its footprint, so no ground comes up through it (the result names what set the floor, and warns when it is not the ground: a loose piece clipping the footprint); where the ground falls away a plinth skirts down to under the lowest ground, as Highpass's houses stand on stone bases. Its parts are not edited by hand (the object, mesh, material, and UV tools refuse them): `editStructure` moves, turns, or re-plinths it, and with no changes lays it again; `removeStructure` takes it back and returns its definition. On a slope a door faces uphill, where the ground meets the floor; the result says how high the step to each threshold is and walks a walk-in building's entrances.
- **Defined structures.** A structure keeps its definition, the kit fingerprints it was laid from, and every ground lookup its lay made; its geometry is always laid again from them, never edited by hand (its parts refuse the object, mesh, material, and UV tools, naming the structure tools). Each lays against the ground as it stands, leaving out its own parts and every structure laid after it, so a flight landing on a walkway stands on the walkway and a later walkway over a bridge leaves the bridge alone. The loop is the artist's: define, lay, look from the views the result gives (save them as review cameras) and walk its walk line (walkRoute and renderRouteStrip take its name), adjust the definition (editStructure), and when the ground or the kit changes, see it stale (getStructures, regradeTerrain's `staleStructures`: the ground under it moved, its lookups finding something else now, named where something stands over the ground; a piece it baked or a prefab's footprint, entrances, or parts changed in its kit; or its kit is missing) and lay it again (editStructure with no changes, in getStructures' order); removeStructure takes one back and returns its definition. Nothing lays again by itself. A span bakes its pieces, so it follows a kit edit only when laid again; placed pieces, wall sections, and buildings follow at once (a window cut in a wall reaches every building that uses it when the zone is next opened, without making it stale).
- **Judging a building.** From the plaza at eye height with the figure (one building, not a heap of pieces; the roof seated with its overhang), all the way round it (renderOrbit), close on the downhill side (the plinth meeting the ground, no floating corner), through the door from outside and from inside looking out with the figure (a walk-in floor, light through the door), a section along the slope through it (floor, plinth, and roof against the ground), and after a kit edit the same cameras with compareRenders.
- **Carved halls** (guild halls are carved into cliffs as terrain, as Crescent's halls and market are): a hall is a cave cut square (`cutCave` with `wallShare` 1): walls straight up the full height under a flat ceiling, square corners, its far end a flat wall inside the rock, its mouth standing wholly in the open in front of the cliff. Trim bands (`trimBands`) run along its walls at set heights over the floor (a dado, a frieze, Crescent's `cr_tile_trim_marble_dark`), the walls cut exactly along their edges, each in its own material mapped along the band, and come back on every cut. The cliff at the mouth is then dressed into a carved face (`dressFacade`): a flat vertical rectangle framing the opening, square to the hall's path (or turned to the cliff's line where the hall meets the cliff obliquely), standing in a recess in the cliff over a level apron, the ground in front easing back to the ground around it, the faces it moves mapped again. Ground in front of the face rising over its top is refused (move the face out toward the cliff's face, or turn it), and so is easing into the ground within another hall's reach. The facade is a defined pass that keeps the face itself, so it is graded again with or without the cave (`regradeTerrain` replays it, then refits the hall to open on it; `removeShapingPass` takes it back and refits the hall), and the hall is refit in the same call that dresses it. Changing a hall (`editCave`) dresses its facades again to fit, or refuses, naming the facade, where one would no longer frame it. A portal kit piece placed at the facade's returned frame (`placeKitPiece`) gives the opening its crisp trim, as Crescent frames its halls with placed columns; the face and apron are painted afterwards. A hall is judged as a cave is, and more: from the apron at eye height with the figure (a flat carved face with straight edges, the opening crisp, no ragged lip), from across the valley (dressed rock in the cliff, not a slab stuck on), inside looking out (bands level as trim, the ceiling flat, the corners square, the back wall flat), in sections along and across, and in texel density and coverage shadings of the face and lining.
- **In the export.** Structures reach the zone file as the client's own do: a piece placed by hand is one model however often it is placed; a building is one model per part (its nested pieces merged), shared by every placement, and a plinth one of its own; a bridge, flight, or walkway is one model, or terrain when it was laid in the terrain collection; a wall places its piece's model where it stands level and one model per distinct shear, shared by every wall in the file, with its posts' model at each post; a carved hall is terrain. Ropes and rails stay passable in the file, as their faces are flagged. A kit's material and the zone's own of one name export apart. Before a game export every stale structure is laid again (a test export lists them to confirm), and a kit that cannot be found refuses both; checkExport gives each structure's models and triangles and the triangles of every placement, which compareWithClientZones sets beside the client's zones (Highpass Hold, Crescent Reach, and Freeport place a quarter to three quarters of a million). A round trip (exportZone, importZoneFile into a new file, the same review cameras saved again, compareRenders) shows nothing missing, no rope lost, no material swapped; the import draws normal-mapped faces a little differently from the authoring materials, the same for any normal-mapped mesh.

### Review

Views come first: renderView at eye height, from above, close, and framed on given objects; views all the way round them (renderOrbit); plans (renderSketch) and sections (renderSection); and the same view before and after a change with what changed (compareRenders). The places worth looking at again are kept as review cameras, each with a note of what to judge there (saveReviewCamera), and rendered together on one sheet after each pass (renderReviewSet), so a change is judged from the same places every time. The places the concept art shows are kept the same way, each camera matched to its painting by laying the art over the render until the horizon and the big forms line up (compareToConcept), and compared with it again after each pass: side by side, squinted into light and dark masses, by their strongest edges, and by palette, with numbers for each. The ways players take are kept as review routes (saveReviewRoute) and walked as a strip of eye-level frames along them, with a frame at every place the walk stops a player (renderRouteStrip). A view names the objects it shows (labels), or draws each object in its own color with a legend (objects shading), so a picture says what is what. Diagnostic shadings show what the eye misses in a lit view: curvature (convex forms warm, concave cool) for reading form, triangle density for where the mesh is too fine or too coarse for its place, and texel density for textures drawn at different scales side by side. After each step a critic judges the review renders with fresh eyes (the zone-critic agent, `.claude/agents/zone-critic.md`): it sees only the pictures, the layout's intents, the concept art, and reference screenshots, holds them to the `review-zone` skill's checklist, and names each problem with where it is and how bad, or passes the view. Checks confirm what the views show: walkRoute for slope, footing, and headroom along a route, compareWithClientZones for construction against the client's zones, and checkExport for texture coverage (below). Planned: checks for props floating above or buried in the ground.

### Export

An export has a purpose. A test export (quick renders, test loads) writes everything designed (swim volumes, boundaries, zone lines, the triangles players pass through) and refuses only what no zone file can hold, listing what a game export would still refuse; a game export (files for the client and server) also refuses what is not yet decided: structures laid on ground or a kit that has changed since, blockout materials on exported faces, pools and rivers without a swim decision, the zone row's view values, the safe point and underworld (with ground under the safe point), zone lines without a target or sharing a number, and containment, which needs reach mapping and so refuses every game export until that exists. checkExport runs the same checks as exportZone, writes nothing, and lists every failure and every finding at once, each with its object, material, image, face count, and where its faces lie; exportZone refuses on any failure, refuses unsaved work, and replaces the side files and then the archive only once everything is written, putting every file back as it was if any step fails. importZoneFile brings an exported archive back, its walls and zone lines as reference and the triangles it lets players through marked so, so a round trip is checked in the same views and with the same walks.

Coverage is judged as the rest of the zone is, in pictures: renderView's coverage shading draws each exported face in its status color (the base material showing where no surfacing layer covers it, a ground border with no transition strip, a texture stretched or squeezed against its material's usual scale, zero texture area, blockout, and anything export cannot write), with faces seen from their back in blue, so the list and the map are read together. Fixes are design edits (paintSurface, paintTransition, projectUVs, assignMaterial, cleanupMesh), each followed by another check and another look; nothing is assigned automatically and there is no fallback texture.

### Surfacing and lighting from the catalog

Textures, light styles, and particle emitters come from the asset catalog ([assetCatalog.md](assetCatalog.md)): surveyed from client zones, looked at on contact sheets, and described once in a shared vocabulary, so a need ("red layered rock for steep faces") finds them and each use starts from how the client's own zones used them. EQ terrain zones' ecosystems are recipes for the surfacing pass: which texture goes on which slopes and heights, at what repeat. Their artists painted which ecosystem covers which area, and the rules apply only inside it.
