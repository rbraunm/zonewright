# Authoring a zone

zonewright gives you an artist's hands; use them as an artist would. The procedural tools (masks, noise, scatter, recipes) are brushes and helpers inside an area you have decided on. They are never the design. A zone surfaced by one slope rule or roughened by one noise pass everywhere reads as generated, because it was. `docs/zoneWorkflow.md` holds the method; this is the working procedure.

## The creed

- **Intent before tools.** Know what each area is to become, from the concept and references, before touching it. Write it down as a region (`createRegion`, with its intent and its access: whether players play there, only see it, or never reach it).
- **Region by region, at any scale.** A large zone is many regions, each with its own forms, palette, and dressing. Global operations are for blocking out only, and are then broken up per region.
- **Recipes per region, decisions by hand.** The client's terrain zones put rock on steep slopes nearly everywhere, but as part of each painted area's recipe: each area has its own palette and slope thresholds, height bands target features (a sulfur band, river beds below the water line, grass above a height), and edges blend softly. Do the same: a slope or height recipe painted inside a region (`paintSurface` with the region and a mask), targeted bands, softened edges; and strokes and boundary edits for everything a rule cannot decide (`paintSurface`, `editSurface`).
- **Rough to fine, judged in pictures after every pass.** Building a zone is visual iteration. Look at the result: `renderView` at eye height (`standAt`), from above (`map` with `shading: layout`), and close on the part being worked from several sides and from where players see it; judge what you see, then change, reduce, or remove. Numbers (`measure`, `walkRoute`, `compareWithClientZones`, a script's samples) check what a picture shows and never stand in for it; sweeps of sampled heights or ray casts are no way to judge a form.
- **Removing is a way of adding.** Mute or turn down passes and layers, take an area back (`resetRegion`, `rebuildRegion`, `clearRegion`, `eraseSurface`), and do it again better.
- **A critic after every step.** When a step below is done, review it by the `review-zone` skill: render the review cameras, the routes, the plan, and the concept comparisons, and have the zone-critic agent judge them with fresh eyes. Fix what it names, render the same cameras again, and run a new critic on them.
- **Sketch when unsure.** When the next step or how to improve an area is unclear (a multi-building layout, a village, a dungeon's rooms, where an arrival point and its buildings go), sketch it in plan (`sketch`, `renderSketch`): footprints with heights and facings, yards, paths, points, and notes, each measured against the ground under it, and footprints standing as blocks in eye-level views. Sketches persist on their sheets and are revised like other work, but they are aids, not the plan the zone is held to: change the sketch or leave it behind as the views say.
- **Steer by the EQ worlds, not taste alone.** `compareWithClientZones` after each pass (named reference zones of a similar kind work best); the asset catalog for what the client's artists used where.

## How the client's own zones are built

Measured over the 201 EQG zones of the RoF2 client (the zone survey's `construction` group):

| Measure | 10th | Median | 90th percentile |
|---|---|---|---|
| Terrain meshes per zone | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| Terrain textures | 4 | 13 | 34 |
| Terrain triangles per 10,000 square units | 8 | 244 | 5,083 |
| Terrain triangles in one- or two-triangle material islands | 0% | 0.06% | 1% |
| Terrain area steeper than 50 degrees | 44% | 72% | 82% |
| Steep area that is terrain rather than placed models | 11% | 86% | 99% |
| Terrain area painted from palette maps or blended | 0% | 0% | 11% |
| Placed objects | 1 | 378 | 2,342 |

One terrain mesh, its walls modeled into it; per-face materials laid out in clean regions whose borders follow edges the artist modeled; a few hundred to a few thousand placements. Large open zones sit at the sparse end of density; the client's busiest are dense where it matters. Painted ground (palette and blend shaders, as Wall of Slaughter does) is the exception and is outside Phase 1.

### How the client's terrain zones use recipes

Over the 52 EQ terrain zones: a median of 5 painted ecosystems per zone (10th to 90th percentile 2 to 10); in 40 of them a slope rule applies on over 90% of tiles, with thresholds varying by ecosystem (Goru'kar Mesa 1, 25, 30, and 43 degrees; Sunderock 8, 40, and 45); height bands target features (Sunderock's sulfur ground between z 340 and 380, its river rocks below set heights; Sunrise Hills' grass above z 500); each rule has a slope and height tolerance and painted cover maps blend the result.

## Procedure

1. **References.** The concept art (`ref/<zone>/concept` in the work repository); reference zones of a similar kind found with the zone survey and looked at with `importZone`; the asset catalog's measured use of candidate textures. Name the reference zones you will compare against. Once the ground is blocked out, match a review camera to each concept painting (`compareToConcept` with `saveAs`), laying the art over the render until its horizon and big forms line up, so every later pass is compared with the art from where the art stands.
2. **Plan (layout).** Block the space out on a coarse grid; then `createRegion` for every area with a distinct intent (each terrace, the bay, each canyon wall section, the river, the plateau rim), each with its access: `play` where players walk or swim, `view` for what they only see (a backdrop slope, a far shore), `none` for what they never reach (behind the rim, rock interiors); every ground players could reach lies in some region, so backdrops get regions of their own. `getRegions` is the plan, its `undecided` list the regions still without access; keep intents and access current with `editRegion`, and look at `renderSketch`, which fills regions by access. Place where players arrive (`placeEntry`): a `zoneIn` for every neighbour's zone line that leads here (`fromZone`, and `fromNumber` when its zone_points number is known) and a `landing` for every port, isolated where only its port reaches; look at each one's arrival view, and at the plan's entries layer, which also draws the safe point, teleport landings, and plot entrances (`getEntries` lists them all and says when one no longer stands on its footing). Decide the zone's housing here (`setZoneHousing`): none, incidental (a few plots in a zone for something else, such as along its main road), featured, or primary; what it is for; world or neighborhood placement; how many plots; how they are priced; and the main routes, from which a plot's prominence is judged. Set the zone's sky, time of day, fog, and view distance (`setZoneProperties` `sky`, `fogOn`, `fogStart`, `fogEnd`, `fogDensity`, `minClip`, `maxClip`; fog hides only the far interior, never the zone's edge, which a rim taller than eye height hides) so every view shows what players will see: a new zone gets the client's `default` sky unless the client's sky files gain one for it, which is an asset request; zonewright never authors a sky.
3. **Primary forms, per region.** Draw rock forms as the client's zones build them: broad planes, straight cliff runs, sharp corners, few strong ledges, the fine detail left to the texture. Carve, fill, and sculpt the big shapes inside each region, drawing cliff lines, slots, mesas, and terraces as outlines with straight runs and jogs (`sculptOutline`) rather than as even offsets of a path; stepped profiles (strata, ledges, terraces) take `conformBreaks` so their edges run clean. Refine the terrain where it is steep or detailed (`subdivide` with a slope selector inside the region, passes collapsed first) before shaping it further. Keep every major form in its own shaping pass. Forms with an underside, which ground shaped from above cannot hold (a natural arch, an overhanging lip, a ledge off a wall), are their own pieces of the same rock (`createRockFromOutline`): built as the rock around them is (its planes, its strata, its top surface), their hidden parts running into the rock they grow from so no gap or lip shows (`ground`), and surfaced with that rock's recipe; whether a form is its own piece or part of the ground depends on the landform. An arch is a mass with a hole in it, not a band across a gap: a thick lintel, an outline that varies in width and widens into the walls, an opening that is lopsided and springs low, and an underside flared up toward the faces so it reads as a broad vault rather than a doorway. A cave is cut into the ground itself (`cutCave`) once the rock it runs into has its primary form: grade the approach to its mouth first (`gradeRoute`) so the cave starts on open ground within a step of its floor, then sweep it along a floor path into the rock, widening into rooms where plots go and ending blind or on open ground again. A covered gallery or a rock shelter runs along a cliff with part of its width in the open: trace its path (`traceLedge`, more than half its width inside the rock) and cut it with walls and floor close in value to the cliff's own rock, so it reads as a ledge under a lip, not a stripe or a ribbon. Look at it from the approach at eye height, close on the throat, from inside looking out, and in sections across and along it (`renderSection`); change it with `editCave` rather than cutting another. Shaping around a cave leaves its lining where it is and its mouth stale (`getObjectDetail` lists stale caves): `editCave` with no changes, or `regradeTerrain`, refits it. Tools that change faces refuse a mesh holding caves: take the cave back (`removeCave`, which returns its definition), make the change, and cut it again.
4. **Water, per body.** Carve a channel or basin first, then lay the water in body by body: a pool flooded to a level (`floodWater`), a river along a path of falling levels (`runWater`; it may enter or leave at the zone's edge), a fall hung from a lip into the pool below (`pourWaterfall`). Each is a starting point: look at it close at the shore, from above, and from where a player first sees it, then adjust it (`editWater` for its level, path, reach, lip, throw, or spread; `shapeWaterExtent` to bound a leak or let it into a cove) and look again. A body's build report names where it reached the end of the ground (a zone edge used on purpose, or a leak) and a fall's rows inside the rock. Deepen a bed under still water with `carveWaterBed`; surface the bed and wet banks with the `underWater` and `nearWater` selectors. Water uses `createLiquidMaterial`: start from a client zone's water of the same kind (its two water colors give its hue, and its DDS cube map what it mirrors) and compare against it; give a river `flow` (or a waterfall ribbon over a rock bed) and check `getWater` says it flows downstream, and a fall downward, with two `renderView`s a second apart (`liquidTime`) when in doubt. Lava is a liquid too: its diffuse's alpha is the crust and the rest glows unlit with its second diffuse, so paint the alpha where it should glow and look at it at night as well as by day. White water is never a surface: spray each fall's foot (and the lip of a tall fall, and splashes laid by hand at points on rapids and where a river joins a pool) with `sprayWater`, using the emitter definitions client zones use there, with ripple rings on the pool (never on dry rock), and place a few wet rocks at the foot by hand to hide where the sheet meets the water; judge it across the pool, at eye level at the foot, from above, and close on the white water, by day and night. Design for swimming but leave the swim volumes until the water and bed settle: then `buildSwimVolumes` per body (or `placeSwimVolume`, `editWater` swimmable false for a fountain), look at them in sections across each body (`renderSection`) and in plan (`renderSketch` with the swim layer), adjust by hand, and `acceptSwimVolumes` after any later change.
5. **Housing, per plot.** Place plots one at a time where the plan puts them (`placePlot`; `layOutPlots` only as a first draft of a street), facing their street, then for each: grade it (`gradePlot`), look at it from the street at eye height and from above with the guides on, `assessPlot` it, set its features (`editPlot`; a view is your judgment from pictures taken at the plot's edge looking out as its owner would, not a measure: a plot deep in a cave has none, and a shelf high up does not have one just for its height), grade the way to its entrance where the ground does not give one (`gradeRoute`: up a steep side, place a switchback's hairpins yourself, keep its legs near the ground, and look along each leg at eye height and in sections across them), and walk from the street to its entrance (`walkRoute`). Vary them as the land does: a plot on a ledge with a view, one secluded in a side canyon, a row along the road, one in a cavern (cut it with a level stretch at least 220 wide and long, place the plot with that stretch's floor as its height, and look at it from its entrance looking in). `getHousing` lists every price and how it was reached; export refuses overlapping plots.
6. **Secondary forms, per region.** Break regularity deliberately: jogs drawn into outlines, rock pressed into facets (`facet`), and only then a little warp or roughen confined to the region (`{"region": name}` with a `fadeDistance`), at the region's own scale; smooth noise everywhere reads as ripples, not rock. Check `foldedFaces`; turn passes down where they overdo it. Shaping over graded routes and pads leaves them off target: `regradeTerrain` puts them back, in the order they were made.
7. **Surfacing, per region.** Choose the palette from the catalog against the concept (`use-assets`). Add a surfacing layer per decision (the region's ground, a stratum band, a path, accents). Lay each region's recipe first (its ground; rock on its steep faces above that region's threshold; height bands for features), then paint by stroke what no rule decides, with `edgeNoise` so edges wander; break a large surface of one material with patches of a second (`paintSurface` with a `noise` selector inside it); pick off specks and pinholes (`editSurface` clean) and smooth, grow, or shrink edges (`editSurface`) so no speckle remains near a threshold; then run each painted border onto modeled edges (`conformSurfaceEdges`), as the client's zones do, so no border zigzags along the triangles. Where a band or strip must end at a set height or distance, cut it there first (`cutContours`). Put transition textures where grounds meet (`cutContours` with `distanceFrom` at the strip's width, then `paintTransition`), measuring from the border that strip belongs to, such as only the floor at a wall's foot. A cave's lining takes the wall and floor materials it was cut with and is left out of every selector that does not name it; paint it with `{"cave": name}` inside the selector, and the strokes are kept with the cave and painted again when it is recut.
8. **Dressing, per region.** Place sets by hand and generated sets inside regions, varied in scale, heading, and tilt, settled on the ground; hundreds of placements is normal for EQ.
9. **Review.** Look first: each area at eye height along its routes, from above, and each feature close from several sides. Then check what the pictures show with `compareWithClientZones` against the reference zones and `walkRoute` (decks, ramps, ledges, the ways into each area: slope, footing, headroom); fix what reads wrong, region by region, and look again. The whole zone then takes a full `review-zone` pass, every review camera and route judged by the zone critic, before it goes to the owner.

## Starting an area over

- `resetRegion`: take shaping passes back in an area, faded at its edge.
- `rebuildRegion`: span the area's heights from its surroundings (a blank slate that already fits), or level it.
- `clearRegion`: all at once, with its surfacing erased (pass the edgeNoise it was painted with to take back the paint that spilled past its edge) and the objects placed in it removed.
- `eraseSurface`, `setSurfaceLayer` (mute), `removeSurfaceLayer`: take surfacing decisions back.
- `removeCave`: take a cave back, leaving the ground as it would be uncut; it returns the cave's definition, to cut it again, and the strokes kept with its lining.

These are how work goes back and forth. Checkpoints are only for recovery: `saveCheckpoint` before a risky change (a broad generator, a topology change, a script through `runPython`), and `restoreCheckpoint` when the work is wrecked beyond what passes, layers, and the region tools take back; it keeps the wrecked state as a checkpoint first.

## What not to do

- One hard slope or height rule and one palette across the whole terrain, with no areas, no bands, and speckled edges at the threshold.
- One noise pass over everything at one scale, or rock left soft and rippled by warps and roughening where it should be planes and edges.
- Many thin ledges at even offsets running parallel to a path; strata belong to heights and come and go.
- Perfect circles, straight lines, and symmetric forms left as made.
- Material edges that follow the grid; speckled faces near a threshold.
- Declaring a pass done without looking at it from where a player stands.
- Judging a form by numbers (sampled heights, ray casts, counts) instead of by looking at it.
- Building at full detail before the layout is settled.
